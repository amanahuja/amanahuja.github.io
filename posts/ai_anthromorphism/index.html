Anthropomorphising AI

Arguments pro/con about anthropomorphising today's AI systems

September 18, 2026 Aman Ahuja artificial-intelligenceAIresponsible-ai

The following is a brief collection of arguments relating to the practice of using human-centered language when discussing today’s AI technologies.

There are a few claims stated below, followed by quotes from folks who have taken a position on those claims. There is, of course, a link to the quote so you can see what they said within the context of their statements; this contentious framing is ALL about context.

Although I’m trying to capture positions from multiple opposing and supporting angles, I should acknowledge my own bias against anthropomorphizing. I’ll add a short section at the end about that.

Claim: “Anthropomorphic or agentive framing can shift perceived responsibility away from humans and institutions”

This claim is not whether anthropomorphic language is philosophically accurate, but whether describing the AI as the actor changes how ordinary people distribute responsibility.

Bender, Inie, Zukerman

"De-anthropomorphizing language talks about computer systems in terms of their functionality … assigns agency to people using systems and not systems, and avoids aggrandizing metaphors about cognition."
-- Bender, Inie, Zukerman

From “De-anthropomorphizing ‘AI’: From wishful mnemonics to accurate nomenclature,” in First Monday (2026).

The authors include Emily M. Bender, Nanna Inie & Peter Zukerman. They say that terminology should describe system functionality while locating agency in the people who build, deploy, and use the systems. “Language matters. How we describe ‘AI’ technology influences how it is perceived, deployed, and trusted.“It is of no surprise to anyone who has worked on governance that language itself is part of the governance challenge. [Supports].

Rumman Chowdhury

"By using lazy language, we have already placed the responsibility of any negative outcomes on the algorithm."
-- Rumman Chowdhury

From Rumman Chowdhury “Moral Outsourcing: Finding the Humanity in AI” in Forbes (September 12, 2017).

Chowdhury says that language that casts the algorithm as the morally relevant actor relocates blame before anyone has examined the actual chain of human decisions.

Also see her TEDxOccidentalCollege talk “Moral Outsourcing: Humanity in the age of AI”(April 2018) in which she remarks that this arrangement lets creators and deployers retain the upside of the technology while distancing themselves from its harms. [Supports].

Peterson & Almor

"Linguistically framing AIs as agents … influences all people to consider the companies which create them less responsible for their mistakes."
-- Peterson & Almor

From “Agentive linguistic framing affects responsibility assignments toward AIs and their creators,” in Frontiers in Psychology (2025).

Their experiment found that framing an AI grammatically as an agent reduced responsibility assigned to the company that created it. Notably, this company-responsibility effect persisted even among participants with greater AI experience.

Also see Victoria Oldemburgo de Mello, Jason Plaks & Michael Inzlicht “The Effects of AI Anthropomorphism on Trust and Responsibility,” in Collabra: Psychology (2026).

Ted Chiang

"Whenever a person delegates a decision to an LLM, they are trying to off-load accountability for that decision."
-- Ted Chiang

“No, Artificial Intelligence Is Not Conscious,” The Atlantic (June 3, 2026)

Chiang discusses another locus of responsibility: the user. His concern is not only that companies can shift blame to AI, but that users can experience delegation to an apparently moral or authoritative system as a transfer of their own responsibility.

Claim: “LLMs are not moral agents.”

The claim is not fluent or apparently reasoned output does not establish the capacities required for moral responsibility.

Ted Chiang (again)

"Even if a software agent were conscious and had the best of intentions, the fact that it cannot accept responsibility for its actions disqualifies it from being a moral agent." -- Ted Chiang

From “No, Artificial Intelligence Is Not Conscious” in The Atlantic (June 3, 2026).

Chiang’s point is that linguistic fluency is not evidence that an LLM can bear moral responsibility. He differentiates the ideas of moral patienthood and moral agency, using human children as an analogy. He points out that Anthropic (the org) wants Claude to be virtuous but doesn’t discuss how it could be held responsible for its actions. [Supports].

For a more formal expression of this idea, see Sparroy and Flenady’s “On not being a robot: AI as a threat to rational agency,” in Inquiry (2026).

I have quotes Chiang twice already on this page, but not in relation to his titular claim that AI is not conscious. These are each separate claims, and the idea that they are all connected is yet another claim.

Joseph Keshet

"Variability introduced by stochastic sampling does not amount to choice or authorship."
-- Joseph Keshet

From “Why Sampling Is Not Choosing: Intentionality, Agency, and Moral Responsibility in Large Language Models” (2026)

Claim: “Language about intention can be useful for predicting AI behavior, even when stronger anthropomorphic language is misleading”

The claim is that person-like vocabulary may be useful as predictive shorthand without committing us to a richer picture of the system as a humanlike subject.

Dwarkesh Patel

"Words are made to help us reason about events happening in the world and make predictions about them. I don’t see the value in rejecting the frames of intention, motivation, and collaboration when describing systems which clearly exhibit behavior described by those concepts...

The best way to understand, predict, and reason about [AI] behavior is still to talk in terms of their desires, beliefs, and reactions to experience."
-- Dwarkesh Patel

From this interview with Ajeya Cotra (2024) and @dwarkesh_sp on twitter (2026).

Patel is pragmatic: using language in this way is acceptable to make a point, even if those terms should not read as statements about consciousness or moral agency. Maybe he would take the “if it talks like a duck” stance.

Simon Coghlan

"People can, of course, use mentalistic language to describe things without believing they are really sentient."
-- Simon Coghlan

From “Anthropomorphizing Machines: Reality or Popular Myth?” in Minds and Machines (2024).

Historical antecedents

Anthropomorphizing computer and algorithms is nothing new, and it’s not the first moment that concerns are being raised. It’s helpful to see the historical context.

From “A Logical Calculus of the Ideas Immanent in Nervous Activity,” in Bulletin of Mathematical Biophysics (1943):

"Because of the ‘all-or-none’ character of nervous activity, neural events and the relations among them can be treated by means of propositional logic."
-- Warren McCulloch & Walter Pitts (1943)

From The Human Use of Human Beings (1954), a discussion of Wiener’s machine ethics:

"To throw the problem of his responsibility on the machine … is to cast his responsibility to the winds."
-- Norbert Wiener (1954)

From “Rival,” The New Yorker (November 29, 1958):

"Our success in developing the perceptron means that for the first time a non-biological object will achieve an organization of its external environment in a meaningful way."
-- Frank Rosenblatt (1958)

From : “Computer Power and Human Reason” (1976):

"I was startled to see how quickly and how very deeply people conversing with DOCTOR became emotionally involved with the computer and how unequivocally they anthropomorphized it."
-- Joseph Weizenbaum (1976)

From “The Intentional Stance” (1987):

"The intentional strategy consists of treating the object whose behavior you want to predict as a rational agent with beliefs and desires."
-- Daniel Dennett (1987)

From “Talking About Large Language Models” (2023):

"... as long as there is no possibility of anyone assigning more weight to such terms than they can legitimately bear, if there is no danger of their use misleading anyone about the character and capabilities of the systems being described.

However, today’s large language models, and the applications that use them, are so powerful, so convincingly intelligent, that such licence can no longer safely be applied.
-- Murray Shanahan

Shanahan notes that these “philosophically-loaded” terms like “knows”, “believes” “thinks”, etc, have been harmless in relation to past technologies. They were “harmless because no-one takes them seriously enough.” But modern LLMs are now good enough that this language is now dangerous to use.

I don’t think he says it explictly, but it is also relevant that this terminology used to be confined to experts in the field, and is now part of the popular conversation. LLMs are being used and discussed by so many people who are not researching or creating them.

Aman’s thoughts

These claims are not mutually exclusive, obviously. One can believe that speaking about agents as having intentions is useful in some contexts and ALSO believe that it displaces moral responsibility and ALSO believe that large language models are not moral agents.

Choosing to use such language is a choice. If you are a human making that choice, it is a moral choice. If an LLM uses such language, there are consequences, and the model’s creators are responsibile.

I personally have long been frustrated by the practice of using human-centered language when discussing today’s technologies. (I still try to avoid saying “artificial intelligence”, although that effort has been increasingly futile).

When asked whether machines might think, Richard Feynman said that a machine will not think in the same way that human think, anymore than an airplane flies in the same way that a bird flies, or a fast vehicle moves like a cheetah. It’s up to us to decide if that matters.

It is doing something, and whether that thing it is doing should be considered to be “thought”, and whether it is impressive, or dangerous, are all separate and important considerations.

All these things are simultaneously true:

  • These technologies are changing the world.
  • They (meaning “these technologies”, without ascribing personhood) are very good at doing a lot of things that I did not think they’d be able to do at all, and continue to get better at an alarming rate.
  • They are bad at doing some things that aren’t hard for some humans.
  • They frequently make mistakes that we need to be more wary of.
  • They are biased and prejudiced.
  • The creators of these systems have direct and enormous determination over the capabilities and biases of these systems, but they may not in the future.
  • These technologies will certainly cause immense damage and disruption, and have already done so.
  • We must govern them and regulate the models. And the systems that use these models, and the industries, and the economies around these technologies. Soon.
  • There is no evidence that these models are conscious, in that they have subjective experiences, or that they self-conscious, in that they are aware of themselves in the Hofstadian sense. They are not sentient, meaning that they do not have felt experiences.
  • They are clearly capable of doing things we didn’t ask or expect them to do. They are constantly doing things we don’t want them to do. This misalignment is increasing rapidly in both magnitude and consequence.
  • We humans are responsible for all the small stuff and all the big stuff.

This could and should be a cleaner and longer list. But I have to go make dinner.
