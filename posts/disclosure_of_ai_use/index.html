Disclosures of AI use

A discussion of a few AI disclosure frameworks

October 9, 2026 Aman Ahuja LLMAIartificial-intelligenceresponsible-ai

I love that more and more people are thinking about how to disclose how they are using AI, and how much. They want to be transparent about it. This push towards transparency is coming from authors, from platforms, and from institutions.

How much of this post was written by me, versus written by an AI model?

I noticed that a colleague, Beau Cronin was using a system he created for himself (beaucronin.com/tail). Soon I was noticing them in more places. I was particularly interested in journalists and writers: many publishers are devising their own approach for disclosure. Searching, I found several attempts to standardize, such as Disclose AI Usage (DAIU ).

My first thought was that I should pick one of these approaches and start using it myself. In reviewing a few of the ones I had encountered, I found

  • They often use scales that aren’t aligned with my interests
  • Adding complexity makes it more accurate, but with a price.
  • This will get more confusing as AI gets better at writing
  • Adding such a disclosure might backfire

So where does that leave me?

1. A scale from little to much

Disclosure methods often introduce a scale from “little AI use” to “lots of AI use”. That seems intuitive, but it requires a hierarchy of AI use.

Consider an example (EXAMPLE A): An author asks an AI to pick a topic for a blog post that will attempt to get the most views, to research the subject, and write an outline of an article with three thought provoking arguments. The author then usese this outline to write the final copy themselves. They pen every word – in their own style and words, with spelling and grammar mistakes.

This feels like a pretty significant use of AI to me. My interpretation is that AI took the lead in informing the topic, the substantive content, and the structure of the argument and/or narrative. Therefore a scale or ranked system should place this example pretty “high”.

This is how this example might show up in TAIL, REAL, and DAIU.

TAIL. Example A would be TAIL 1, which suggests minimal use of AI instead of significant use.

REAL. The REAL framework (https://arxiv.org/html/2610.04021v1) recognizes that “ideas” are a form of contribution. Example A would land in Level 2 (“Brainstorming”). If the author deemed the AI to be a co-author, it would be Level 4. If I was the author, I might consider those contributions substantial, but the guidance isn’t precise about this.

DAIU. The DAIU standard differentiates “Content Origin” such that my Example A would be categorized as a “AG” / “AI Collaborator”, if we agree that drafting the outline qualifies as a draft. I think it does. The AI contributions seem to land on category “Major” (which is numerically 3 in a scale from 0-4). so the ranking would be AG-Major/3. (Notably, ChatGPT selected AG-Remix/2, but I disagree).

What if we reverse this fabricated example, such that the AI contributes only the copy? The human comes up with the topic, spends an amount of time on desk research appropriate to the topic, comes up with a narrative structure and the key arguments. Then the author hands a “spec” to an AI agent. The AI agent writes every word of the blog post, which the human then reads and approves, for the sake of argument no revision are required. Let’s call this Example Z.

I asked an AI to consider these examples, and it provided this commentary:

In Example A, the author could produce a beautifully idiosyncratic piece
of writing, rich in personal voice, without contributing a single original
idea.
In Example Z, the author might produce a generic, unmistakably
AI-sounding article despite having done all the intellectually demanding work. 
...
Example A could look human while thinking like AI. Example Z could look
like AI while thinking like a human.

Haha, okay, okay.

This is how Example Z would rank in those systems:

  • TAIL: TAIL 4 or TAIL 5. Probably 4, because the author could confidently “stand by” the specific output.
  • REAL: Level 5. AI contributions to prose seems to tip the scales, leading perhaps to a “Level 3”, even if the ideas were the authors.
  • DAIU: “HM-Major”. Again, this depends on what the framework considers to be the first draft. Is the spec or outline a draft? This could be AG-Major or HM-Major, depending.

I find this to be a little troubling about these ranking systems. Example Z seems to be a more significant use of AI than Example A in these three systems, at least for what I had in mind: writing an article, a whitepaper, or a blog post.

However, what if instead we consider a poem or a play? In that case a thorough outline (or “specification”) contains merely plot and structure, but the importance of the final prose cannot be understated. The style and choice of work become much more important to the whole. Likewise if we consider a drawing, a musical composition, or an animation.

Code, poem, research paper, a math proof. The significance of AI contribution depends on the context.

Multidimensional and narrative disclosure

The DAIU approach of indicating both level and origin is interesting. One could imagine adding more dimensions.

“How much did AI contribute to …”

  • … editorial: selection of topic and audience strategy
  • … intellectual : origin of arguments and structure
  • … expression: language, diction, prose
  • … research: fact checking, citations, verification

This starts to get complex really quickly. Simplicity seems more important than precision, when providing a disclosure to a casual reader who isn’t immersed in these topics.

Maybe a scale isn’t necessary at all. The team at Intertidal shared a different approach, from Elsevier. The system that Elsevier uses doesn’t a numeric score at all. They provide editorial guidelines on acceptable use of AI, and disclosure requirements. Authors following Elsevier’s policy are asked to describe how they used AI.

Elsevier policy might not allow Example A at all, as it raises a authorship-integrity concern. Example Z under this policy might say something like:

During the preparation of this manuscript, the author used Anthropic Opus-5 to generate the article’s text from a detailed author-developed specification. The author independently conducted the research and developed the article’s analysis, structure, arguments, and conclusions. The AI-generated manuscript was reviewed and approved by the author, who takes full responsibility for its content.

I don’t claim to be an expert on these policies, but it is significant that Elsevier’s policy might not allow Example A at all – a scenario which in other disclosure systems was ranked low.

The “AI Smell” is a-changing.

Automated AI detectors are fighting an arms race in trying to detect when content is generated by AI. I don’t know how they work, but they seem to be looking for tells – for statistical patterns in the style and the writing. These might include for diction, style, even sentence structures or paragraph structures. I’m sure it gets more complex than that, and at some point it’s AIs detecting AIs (or, AIs providing statistical estimates of whether some writing is similar to that produced by AI.)

Substack is trying out an automated feature that flags AI-generated writing using Pangram (which, BTW, sparked some backlash from authors on the platform).

These products must have their hands full keeping up-to-date with model releases, and with an ever-increasing variety of models.

Aside: Evidence is mixed about how AI Disclosure policies might affect
readers. Research by trustingnews suggests that people want disclosures
from newsrooms in general, but mistrust a specific article if it has an
AI-use disclosure.

The most frustrating AI contribution today isn’t the proverbial em-dash. Not anymore. It’s the type of writing that sounds poignant but is lacking in substance. That’s a lot harder to detect than some of the “tells” listed in Wikipedia: “Signs of AI writing”.

Possibely soon in the future, both of these will be gone: the AI “smell”, the current superficial kind and the next deeper one, will not show up in these models output.

Then we have to ask the question, why do we care if it’s AI or human? We will still care, but we’ll need to think carefully about why. Recent discussions about AI math proofs had split opinions – some mathematicians said that if the proof is sound, authorship doesn’t matter.

I will still want to know when a colleague sends me a message that an AI wrote for them, or when an essay by an admired writer contains astute observations that aren’t their own. A poem written by a human will always be imporant, even if someone like me can no longer spot the AI in a double-blind poetry reading.

So what should Aman do?

I’ll disclosure THIS post using ALL the systems.

  • TAIL: TAIL 2
  • REAL: AI Influence Level 2 “Brainstorming”.
  • AI Flags: B - AI Assisted.
  • DAIU: HM-REMIX/2.

I am giving weight to the fact that I used AI to help “rank” or evaluate fabricated examples. I reviewed the two A/Z examples I ended up including, but didn’t independently review all the fabricated examples.

I include AI-generated text directly in a pullquote, clearly attributed. I consider that to be like other quotations, and not direct authorship of this blog’s text.

(By the way, I had previously neglected to directly review just one of the frameworks. I went back and reviewed it specifically to keep this post’s disclosure ranking lower. Maybe I wouldn’t have done that otherwise. Thus including an AI disclosure forced me to be more thorough.)

Let me attempt the Elsevier disclosure as well:

**AI use disclosure (Elsevier-style)**
This article was written with assistance from ChatGPT. I developed the
central thesis, examples, narrative structure, arguments, and conclusions,
and wrote the prose.

I used ChatGPT to research AI disclosure frameworks, explore their
implications, test hypothetical scenarios, and suggest interpretations and
classifications. I evaluated these suggestions, sometimes disagreed with
them, and retained my own judgments. The article contains an explicitly
attributed quotation from ChatGPT.

I reviewed the AI-generated contributions and take responsibility for the
article's content.

I find the narrative disclosure to be the most helpful and most attractive. I can see myself using something like that for longer posts, and maybe a different simpler one for short posts.

If you know of a disclosure system you like better than the ones I’ve mentioned, I’d love to hear about it.
